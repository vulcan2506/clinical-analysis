## Nutrient Reference Standards

These nutrient reference standards address biochemical measurements critical for assessing vitamin status, focusing on vitamin D and vitamin B12. They highlight assay specificity, physiological influences, and clinical thresholds for optimal function.

**Key Features:**
- Vitamin D 25(OH)D assay measures both D2 and D3 metabolites with sensitivity to environmental and physiological factors
- Vitamin B12 reference range is defined as 211.00–911.00 pg/mL

**Notable Details:**
- Optimal calcium absorption requires 25(OH)D levels >75 nmol/L
- 25(OH)D levels vary seasonally by 40–50% between winter and summer
- 25(OH)D is influenced by sunlight exposure, latitude, skin pigmentation, sunscreen use, and hepatic function
- Vitamin B12 is measured in pg/mL

- Clinical guidelines suggest that Vitamin B12 is necessary for making red blood cells, DNA, maintaining nerve cell health, and releasing energy from food, and deficiency symptoms include anaemia, fatigue, sore/red tongue, muscle weakness, nerve damage, depression, and memory loss (*Dietary advice for Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.pdf*).

- Clinical guidelines suggest that patients on acid-suppressing medications may need fortified foods or supplements for adequate B12 intake (*Dietary advice for Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.pdf*).

- Clinical guidelines suggest that patients with suspected pernicious anaemia should undergo testing for intrinsic factor antibodies or undergo a Schilling test to confirm diagnosis (*Dietary advice for Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.pdf*).

**Diagnostic Criteria:**
- Clinical guidelines suggest that Vitamin B12 deficiency can be evaluated through clinical assessment for deficiency symptoms and laboratory tests such as methylmalonic acid and homocysteine (*Dietary advice for Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.pdf*).



## Renal and Cardiometabolic Risk

These guidelines emphasize the critical role of kidney function assessment in managing dyslipidemia and overall cardiometabolic risk. They highlight standardized testing methods, contextual interpretation of results, and the need for prompt action based on findings.

**Key Features:**
- Kidney function assessment in cardiovascular risk stratification for dyslipidemia management
- Use of standardized serum creatinine testing methods (Modified Jaffe, Kinetic) for accurate evaluation
- Immediate patient contact for unexpected or alarming kidney function results
- Interpretation of serum creatinine in the context of age, sex, and muscle mass

**Notable Details:**
- Serum creatinine must be measured using validated laboratory methods (Modified Jaffe, Kinetic)
- Unexpected kidney function results require immediate remedial action and patient contact
- Age, sex, and muscle mass must be considered for accurate kidney function assessment

**Significance Level:** MODERATE

**Key Clinical Actions:**  
- Assess kidney function (e.g., serum creatinine) as part of cardiovascular risk stratification in dyslipidemia management.  
- Perform serum creatinine testing using standardized methods (e.g., Modified Jaffe, Kinetic) to evaluate kidney function.  
- Unexpected or alarming kidney function results should prompt immediate patient contact for remedial action.  

- Clinical guidelines suggest using enzymatic creatinine assays where possible, as they reduce interference from non‑creatinine chromogens (Chronic Kidney Disease.pdf).  
- Guideline context: Laboratories should ensure assay traceability to JCTLM‑listed reference materials and document the filtration marker and equation used on the report (Chronic Kidney Disease.pdf).  
- Clinical guidelines advise reporting eGFR alongside serum creatinine, rounded to the nearest whole number and indexed to a body surface area of 1.73 m² in adults (Chronic Kidney Disease.pdf).  
- Guideline context: eGFR values <60 mL/min/1.73 m² should be flagged as low in adults to alert providers to possible kidney disease (Chronic Kidney Disease.pdf).  

**Patient Preparation / Diagnostic Criteria:**  
- Serum creatinine must be measured using validated laboratory methods (e.g., Modified Jaffe, Kinetic) for accurate kidney function assessment.  
- Serum creatinine must be interpreted in the context of patient's age, sex, and muscle mass for accurate kidney function assessment.  

- Clinical guidelines require that serum/plasma be separated from red blood cells by centrifugation within 12 hours of venipuncture to prevent creatinine increase over time in Jaffe assays (Chronic Kidney Disease.pdf).  
- Guideline context: When cystatin C is measured, creatinine should be measured on the same sample to enable eGFRcr‑cys calculation (Chronic Kidney Disease.pdf).  
- Guideline context: Quality control procedures should include the lowest expected creatinine range for the population being tested (e.g., infants, small children) (Chronic Kidney Disease.pdf).



## Edge Cases & Standalone Topics

This set of guidelines addresses nuanced aspects of thyroid function testing, emphasizing circadian variation, protein binding effects, and the clinical interpretation of TSH and thyroid hormone levels, including rare variants and new testing methodologies.

**Key Features:**
- TSH circadian variation (50% fluctuation, peaking 2-4 a.m., lowest 6-10 p.m.)
- Total T3/T4 sensitivity to binding protein changes (e.g., pregnancy, steroids)
- Free T3/T4 as biologically active and clinically superior to total hormone levels
- Clinical correlation required for TSH <0.05 μIU/mL due to rare TSH variants
- Introduction of Vitamin D, Ultrasensitive by LC-MS/MS as a new test

**Notable Details:**
- TSH levels vary by ~50% due to circadian rhythm
- Total T3/T4 are affected by thyroid hormone binding protein changes
- Free T3/T4 better correlate with clinical status than total hormone levels
- TSH values <0.05 μIU/mL require clinical correlation due to rare TSH variants
