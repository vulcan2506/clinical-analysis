**Key Features:**

- Chemoradiotherapy Utility: Recommended for unresectable or gross residual DTC to improve locoregional control despite potential toxicity.

- Survivorship Care: Encompasses patients free of disease, those with treatment sequelae, and those with active tumors.
- Shared Decision-Making: Essential for determining initial therapy goals, including active surveillance and long-term treatment plans.
- Vocal Fold Monitoring: Routine pre- and immediate postoperative fiberoptic laryngoscopy recommended to assess motion impairment.
- Surgical Management for Low-Risk DTC: Lobectomy standard for tumors ≤2 cm without gross extra-thyroidal extension or metastases.
- Long-term Complication Management: Requires continuous monitoring and appropriate referrals for treatment-related side effects.
- Preoperative Consent Requirements: Must include discussions on voice/laryngeal, RLN/EBSLN, parathyroid gland management, and drainage.

- MKI monitoring frequency: every 2 weeks initially, then every 1-2 months.

- Serum Tg is not predictive of recurrence in patients following thyroid lobectomy.

### Thyroidectomy vs Lobectomy Outcomes

Lobectomy is preferred for low-risk DTC due to significantly lower complication rates and better quality-of-life measures compared to total thyroidectomy.

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Thyroidectomy vs Lobectomy Outcomes

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Lobectomy is preferred for low-risk DTC due to significantly lower complication rates and better quality-of-life measures compared to total thyroidectomy.
- Total thyroidectomy is generally recommended for clinically advanced primary tumors (cT3-4), nodal involvement (cN1), or distant metastasis (cM1) to facilitate RAI and biomarker surveillance.
- Completion thyroidectomy may be considered for patients with high-risk features identified postoperatively, such as multifocality, contralateral nodularity, or pN1a disease.
- Patient preference and multidisciplinary decision-making are critical in determining the extent of initial thyroidectomy.
- Neoadjuvant systemic therapy may be considered for large, locally invasive DTC with high-risk mutations to improve resectability.
- Patients with larger cT2N0M0 classical PTC may prefer total thyroidectomy and RAI to potentially reduce recurrence risk and improve survival.
- Deferral of completion thyroidectomy for multifocality/contralateral nodularity has little to no impact on survival with appropriate sonographic surveillance.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Low-risk DTC must be clinically small, unilateral, intrathyroidal, and without evidence of regional or distant metastasis for lobectomy to be preferred.
- High-risk mutations (e.g., BRAFV600E with TERT promoter/TP53) may warrant total thyroidectomy in select cases.
- Gross extrathyroidal extension or clinically evident nodal metastasis (cN1a) during lobectomy necessitates conversion to total thyroidectomy.
- Multifocality with clinically significant contralateral nodularity may favor total thyroidectomy to reduce recurrence risk.
- Preoperative clinical risk assessment should guide the extent of surgery, with high-risk features (e.g., age, male sex, family history) influencing decisions.
- For lobectomy to be preferred, the primary tumor must be clinically small, unilateral, intrathyroidal, and without evidence of regional or distant metastasis.
- Gross extrathyroidal extension mandates conversion of lobectomy to total thyroidectomy for complete tumor resection.


### MKI Treatment Adverse Event Prevention

Evaluate patients at baseline and monitor no less often than every 2 weeks for the first 2 months of MKI therapy, then generally at 1- or 2-month intervals thereafter

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** MKI Treatment Adverse Event Prevention

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Evaluate patients at baseline and monitor no less often than every 2 weeks for the first 2 months of MKI therapy, then generally at 1- or 2-month intervals thereafter
- Implement proactive supportive care, including timely identification and management of adverse events, to minimize dose interruptions, reductions, and optimize treatment adherence
- Educate patients on preventive measures for specific adverse events (e.g., daily home blood pressure monitoring for hypertension, symptom diaries for diarrhea, and lifestyle modifications for HFS)
- Monitor TSH, free T4, serum electrolytes, blood counts, proteinuria, and QTc prolongation periodically during MKI therapy
- Consider baseline cardiac function assessment in high-risk patients (e.g., those with hypertension, coronary artery disease, or diabetes)
- Remove hyperkeratotic areas and calluses on hands and feet before treatment initiation and during therapy
- Apply 10% urea-based cream for HFS prevention and 20–40% urea-based creams for emerging HFS

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Blood pressure must be well controlled prior to initiating MKI therapy
- Baseline echocardiogram or other cardiac function study should be considered in selected high-risk patients
- Patients with certain comorbid illnesses (e.g., uncontrolled hypertension, recent acute cardiac syndrome, or bleeding disorders) may be precluded from safely receiving VEGFR MKIs
- Thyroid hormone replacement therapy must be adjusted to maintain appropriate TSH suppression during MKI therapy
- Prophylactic measures (e.g., urea-based creams, dietary adjustments) should be initiated before or at the start of MKI therapy to prevent HFS and diarrhea
- EKG evaluation is required before initiating treatment to assess for QTc prolongation
- Hyperkeratotic lesions and calluses must be removed before treatment initiation to prevent exacerbation of HFS


### Prophylactic Central Neck Dissection

Prophylactic Central Neck Dissection (pCLND). Prophylactic central-compartment lymph node dissection should not be performed for most small, noninvasive, clinically node-negative PTC (cT1-T2, cN0) and for most FTCs.

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Prophylactic Central Neck Dissection (pCLND)

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Prophylactic central-compartment lymph node dissection should not be performed for most small, noninvasive, clinically node-negative PTC (cT1-T2, cN0) and for most FTCs.
- Prophylactic central-compartment neck dissection may be considered in patients with PTC and clinically uninvolved lymph nodes (cN0) who have advanced primary tumors (T3 or T4) or for whom the information will be used to plan further steps in therapy.
- Expectant management of the central compartment when a patient is deemed cN0 is prudent.
- Compartmental dissection of lymph nodes known or suspected to be involved with DTC provides a recurrence-free advantage.
- Therapeutic central-compartment (Level VI and upper Level VII) neck dissection should accompany thyroidectomy for patients with clinically involved central nodes (cN1a).
- Therapeutic central-compartment lymph node dissection is recommended to accompany lateral-compartment neck dissection and thyroidectomy for patients with clinically involved lateral neck lymph nodes (cN1b).
- Therapeutic lateral neck compartmental lymph node dissection (Levels IIa, III, IV, and Vb) should be performed for patients with biopsy-proven or clinically obvious metastatic lateral compartment cervical lymphadenopathy.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Patient must have clinically node-negative PTC (cN0) or FTC.
- Advanced primary tumors (T3 or T4) may warrant consideration for pCLND.
- Information from pCLND must be used to plan further steps in therapy.

- Postoperative neck massage and range-of-motion exercises to mitigate neck tightness/dysphagia.
- Avoid total thyroidectomy if recurrent laryngeal nerve injury occurs during initial lobectomy.

### Reoperation for Locoregional Recurrence

Reoperation for Locoregional Recurrence + Nonsurgical Local Therapies. Reoperation is prioritized for clinically apparent, macroscopic nodal disease identified via imaging rather than serum Tg elevation. Transdisciplinary coordination and patient preference are central to decision-making

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Reoperation for Locoregional Recurrence + Nonsurgical Local Therapies

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Reoperation is prioritized for clinically apparent, macroscopic nodal disease identified via imaging rather than serum Tg elevation
- Transdisciplinary coordination and patient preference are central to decision-making
- Compartmental dissection is recommended over 'berry-picking' to reduce recurrence risk
- Percutaneous ethanol ablation and RFA are reserved for poor surgical candidates or as adjuncts
- RAI therapy may be considered post-local therapy or if local therapy is infeasible, but evidence is low quality
- Reoperative central neck dissection should include at least one paratracheal region with prelaryngeal and pretracheal sub-compartments, while bilateral central neck dissection is reserved for bilateral recurrence due to risks of bilateral recurrent nerve injury and permanent hypoparathyroidism
- Revision lateral neck dissection typically involves Levels II, III, IV, and sometimes V, especially if prior compartmental dissection was incomplete

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Cytological confirmation of disease is generally advised unless findings won’t change management
- High-resolution anatomical studies (ultrasound, CT with contrast, 18FDG-PET/CT, or RAI-SPECT/CT) are mandatory for preoperative planning
- Ultrasound-guided FNA with Tg measurement should confirm nodal recurrence when feasible
- Disease target must be clearly defined with a technical goal for reoperation
- Expertise in the technique is required for nonsurgical therapies (ethanol ablation, RFA)
- Bilateral central neck dissection is advised only when required by recurrence on both sides of the neck due to risks of bilateral recurrent nerve injury and permanent hypoparathyroidism
- Percutaneous ethanol ablation and RFA should ideally be performed for disease that is cytologically proven based on FNA when feasible

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Post-Thyroidectomy Voice Assessment Protocol

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Voice assessment should occur after surgery based on patient's subjective report and physician's objective assessment in the office.
- Early detection of vocal fold motion abnormalities after thyroidectomy is important for facilitating prompt intervention.
- Management of recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) paralysis may include voice therapy, vocal fold injection techniques, and open vocal fold medialization.
- Rates of vocal fold paralysis after thyroid surgery can only be assessed by laryngeal exam postoperatively.
- Massage and neck range of motion exercises in the immediate post-op period may help minimize neck tightness, globus sensation, or dysphagia.
- Surgeon communication with clinical teams should include a multidisciplinary data set of essential perioperative information requiring documentation and communication, as defined by Carty et al.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Collaboration between pathologist and physician specializing in voice is recommended.
- Laryngeal exam is required to assess vocal fold paralysis postoperatively.
- Documentation of recurrent laryngeal nerve identification and preservation in operative notes.
- Pathology reports must include essential histopathologic features for risk assessment, including status of resection margins, vascular invasion, lymph node status, and extranodal extension.
- Histopathologic subtypes associated with unfavorable or favorable outcomes should be identified and reported.
- Surgeon should relay information regarding completeness of resection and reconcile pathology findings (e.g., involved margins, R0 vs. R1 vs. R2 classification) to inform adjuvant treatments and monitoring.


### Preoperative Consent for Thyroidectomy

Preoperative Consent for Thyroidectomy + Voice/Laryngeal Assessment + RLN/EBSLN Management + Parathyroid Gland Management + Drainage

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Preoperative Consent for Thyroidectomy + Voice/Laryngeal Assessment + RLN/EBSLN Management + Parathyroid Gland Management + Drainage

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Explicit preoperative consent must include risks of temporary/permanent nerve injury (voice change, swallowing disability, aspiration risk, tracheostomy), hypoparathyroidism, anesthesia risks, bleeding, scarring, recurrence, reoperation, thyroid hormone supplementation, and surveillance.
- All patients undergoing thyroid surgery should undergo voice assessment (patient description + physician evaluation) as part of preoperative physical examination.
- Preoperative laryngeal exam is mandatory for patients with dysphonia, prior cervical/upper chest surgery, or thyroid cancer with posterior extrathyroidal extension/extensive nodal metastases.
- Visual identification of RLN during thyroidectomy is the gold standard; intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (NIM) is conditionally recommended for malignancy, total/reoperative thyroidectomy, and to assess RLN integrity before contralateral lobe removal.
- Parathyroid glands must be preserved during thyroidectomy; devascularized/removed glands should be auto-transplanted into muscle after frozen section confirmation of benign tissue.
- The preoperative consent process should include discussion of the operating surgeon's own complication rates to ensure transparency and allow patients to make informed decisions based on real-world data.
- Preoperative voice assessment should include patient-reported vocal changes and physician’s objective evaluation, documented in medical records, and may incorporate validated QoL and auditory perceptual assessment voice instruments.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Preoperative voice assessment must include patient-reported vocal changes and physician’s objective evaluation, documented in medical records.
- Preoperative laryngeal exam is required for patients with preoperative dysphonia, prior neck/chest surgery, or thyroid cancer with posterior extrathyroidal extension/extensive nodal metastases.
- Intraoperative RLN identification via visual or NIM is required during thyroidectomy/paratracheal node dissection.
- Postoperative parathyroid hormone-directed calcium/vitamin D supplementation is recommended after total thyroidectomy, central lymph node dissection, or unilateral operations following prior contralateral resections.
- Formal postoperative voice assessment is required if voice abnormalities are present.
- Preoperative laryngeal exam is required for patients with prior external beam radiation to the neck, even if voice is normal.
- Preoperative laryngeal exam is required for patients with a history of prior neck surgery placing the vagus nerve at risk (e.g., carotid endarterectomy, cervical esophagectomy, anterior cervical spine approach), even if voice is normal.

### Tumor Progression Outcomes

Tumor progression outcomes in ablation vs. surgery for differentiated thyroid cancer. Local tumor progression observed in 4.3% of patients after RFA with mean time to progression of 21.5 months. Disease-free survival varies by tumor size (T1a vs. T1b), number of tumors (unifocal vs. multifocal), and subcapsular location (≤2 mm or >2 mm from capsule/trachea)

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Tumor progression outcomes in ablation vs. surgery for differentiated thyroid cancer

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Local tumor progression observed in 4.3% of patients after RFA with mean time to progression of 21.5 months
- Disease-free survival varies by tumor size (T1a vs. T1b), number of tumors (unifocal vs. multifocal), and subcapsular location (≤2 mm or >2 mm from capsule/trachea)
- No significant differences in outcomes between unifocal and multifocal cT1a PTC with RFA in one study (complete disappearance 89% vs. 96%)
- Better outcomes for unifocal vs. multifocal PTC (HR 0.5) and T1a vs. T1b PTC (HR 0.4) in another study
- Percutaneous ablation (RFA/MWA/ethanol) may be an alternative to surgery or active surveillance in selected patients with no significant differences in disease progression or RFS over 5 years
- Complete tumor resolution in 100% of lesions with MWA for multifocal cT1a PTC in a 5-year follow-up study
- Median tumor volume reduction of 80–90% with percutaneous ethanol ablation for cT1aN0M0 PTC

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Tumor size ≤ T1b (≤2 cm) for consideration of ablation
- Unifocal or multifocal cT1aN0M0 papillary thyroid cancer
- Patient selection based on tumor location (e.g., subcapsular proximity) and risk stratification
- Follow-up duration of at least 5 years to assess progression outcomes
- Availability of experienced operators for percutaneous ablation techniques
- Tumors adjacent to the tracheoesophageal groove or 'danger triangle' may be considered for RFA with comparable safety and efficacy to non-adjacent tumors

### Surgeon Volume and Outcomes

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Surgeon Volume and Outcomes in Thyroidectomy

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Higher surgeon volume is associated with improved clinical outcomes and reduced complications in thyroidectomy.
- Low-volume surgeons have higher rates of incomplete resection and compromised outcomes.
- Surgeon volume significantly influences the risk of permanent hypoparathyroidism, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, and hematoma.
- Total thyroidectomy is associated with increased risk of complications for both low- and high-volume surgeons.
- Patients treated at low-volume centers experience higher rates of incomplete resection.
- Surgeon volume-outcome relationships are influenced by both surgeon-specific factors (e.g., technical proficiency, decision-making) and hospital-level factors (e.g., multidisciplinary support, infrastructure).
- Low-volume surgeons are more likely to perform less extensive lymph node dissections in differentiated thyroid cancer, potentially compromising oncologic outcomes.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Surgeons should meet minimum annual thyroidectomy volume thresholds to optimize patient outcomes.
- High-risk thyroid cancer patients should be referred to high-volume surgeons.
- Surgeon training and experience should be considered in the management of differentiated thyroid cancer.
- Surgeons performing thyroidectomy should have access to multidisciplinary teams (e.g., endocrinology, radiology, pathology) to optimize outcomes.
- High-volume surgeons should demonstrate proficiency in managing complex cases, including reoperative thyroid surgery and central neck dissection.


### Surgical Management for Low-Risk Thyroid Cancer

Thyroid lobectomy is the initial surgical procedure for thyroid cancer ≤2 cm without gross extra-thyroidal extension (cT1) and without metastases (cN0M0).

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Surgical Management for Low-Risk Thyroid Cancer

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Thyroid lobectomy is the initial surgical procedure for thyroid cancer ≤2 cm without gross extra-thyroidal extension (cT1) and without metastases (cN0M0).
- For unilateral thyroid cancer >2 and ≤4 cm (cT2N0M0), thyroid lobectomy may be preferred due to lower risk and side effects, but total thyroidectomy may be adopted based on disease features, suspicious contralateral nodularity, or patient preferences.
- Counsel patients about the possibility of conversion to total thyroidectomy or need for subsequent completion thyroidectomy if higher-risk factors emerge intraoperatively or postoperatively.
- Total thyroidectomy is associated with significantly higher complication rates (e.g., recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, hypocalcemia, hemorrhage) compared to thyroid lobectomy.
- Surgeon experience influences complication rates, with higher-volume surgeons having lower risks for both lobectomy and total thyroidectomy.
- Accurate preoperative ultrasound and targeted use of frozen section can help identify patients best treated with total thyroidectomy, reducing the need for subsequent completion thyroidectomy.
- Total thyroidectomy necessitates lifelong thyroid hormone replacement, while lobectomy is associated with postoperative biochemical hypothyroidism (estimated 22% average) and clinical overt hypothyroidism (4% average).

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Thyroid cancer ≤2 cm without gross extra-thyroidal extension (cT1) and without metastases (cN0M0) for initial thyroid lobectomy recommendation.
- Unilateral thyroid cancer >2 and ≤4 cm (cT2N0M0) for consideration of thyroid lobectomy as preferred initial treatment.
- Preoperative FNA biopsy diagnostic for DTC (typically conventional PTC) to inform surgical planning.
- Multidisciplinary team coordination preoperatively to align surgery with overall treatment strategy and follow-up plan.
- Patient preferences and disease features (e.g., suspicious contralateral nodularity) should guide the choice between lobectomy and total thyroidectomy for cT2N0M0 tumors.
- Preoperative identification of multifocality or suspicious contralateral nodules via ultrasound-guided FNA to inform surgical planning for cT1b-T2N0M0 tumors.

## Thyroid Cancer Surveillance & Management

This collection of guidelines outlines a comprehensive clinical framework for the surveillance and management of Differentiated Thyroid Cancer (DTC), spanning from preoperative staging to long-term post-treatment monitoring. The strategy emphasizes risk-stratified approaches, utilizing biochemical markers and advanced imaging to balance recurrence detection with cost-effective, individualized care.

**Key Features:**
- Active Surveillance Protocol: Monitoring low-risk DTC via serial neck ultrasound every 6–12 months instead of upfront surgery.
- Post-Treatment Surveillance Strategy: Utilizing ultrasound 6–12 months post-therapy to evaluate the thyroid bed and lymph nodes.
- Serum Thyroglobulin Monitoring: Quantitative assessment of Tg and TgAb using BCR457-calibrated assays to monitor response.
- Preoperative Imaging for Staging: Use of ultrasound, CT, and F-18 FDG PET/CT to map nodal disease and extrathyroidal extension.
- TSH Suppression Management: Individualized TSH targets based on risk, noting reduced benefit for low-risk patients with low pre-ablation Tg.
- Biomarker Testing for RAIR DTC: Tissue-based testing for oncogenic drivers in radioiodine-refractory progressive disease.
- Preoperative Genomic Evaluation: Not routinely recommended, though specific profiles may inform surgical extent.

## Thyroid Cancer Therapies

This collection of clinical guidelines outlines the multi-modal management strategies for differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC), specifically focusing on radioiodine-refractory (RAIR) disease. It encompasses systemic therapies, targeted molecular interventions, local ablative techniques, and specialized management for metastatic complications.

**Key Features:**
- Radioiodine (RAI) Therapy: Utilized for metastatic disease with dosimetry-guided activities >5.5 GBq and requires low-iodine diet preparation.
- Multi-Kinase Inhibitors (MKI): Lenvatinib is the preferred first-line systemic therapy, while Cabozantinib is recommended for second-line use.
- Targeted Molecular Therapies: BRAF-directed therapy is indicated for BRAFV600E mutations, and MAPK inhibition is considered for redifferentiation.
- Immunotherapy: Pembrolizumab or other ICIs may be considered for patients with high tumor mutational burden or dMMR.
- External Beam Radiotherapy (EBRT): Indicated for locoregionally advanced or recurrent DTC with unresectable or gross residual disease.
- Local Symptomatic Treatment: Includes surgery, radiotherapy, and percutaneous thermo-ablative approaches for symptomatic RAIR DTC.
- Bone Metastasis Management: Use of bone-modifying agents every 3 months to reduce skeletal-related events.
- Brain Metastasis Management: Primarily managed via resection or SBRT, with RAI considered only if lesions concentrate iodine.
- Monitoring Protocols: Asymptomatic RAIR DTC requires TSH-suppressive therapy and serial imaging every 3–12 months using RECIST 1.1.

**Notable Details:**
- Low-iodine diet requires restriction to <50 mcg/day for 1–2 weeks prior to RAI.
- Lenvatinib toxicity includes hypertension, stomatitis, proteinuria, arthralgia, and dysgeusia.
- Systemic treatment selection should prioritize gene-specific therapy (RET or NTRK) before general MKIs.
- SBRT and glucocorticoids are recommended prior to RAI for CNS metastases to mitigate inflammatory responses.
- Indeterminate Tg response (<10 ng/mL stimulated or <5 ng/mL rhTSH) requires thyroid hormone therapy alone.
- Cabozantinib targets VEGFR2, c-MET, and RET kinases.
- MAPK pathway blockade is an emerging approach for redifferentiation in RAI-refractory cases.

## Thyroid Cancer Risk Stratification

The provided themes outline a comprehensive and evolving framework for the risk stratification of Differentiated Thyroid Cancer (DTC), moving from traditional histomorphology toward a multi-dimensional approach. This modern paradigm integrates histopathological features, molecular and genomic markers, and patient-specific factors to guide personalized management and monitoring.

**Key Features:**
- ATA 2025 Risk Stratification: Utilizes tumor type and surgical completeness (R0, R1, R2) to determine recurrence risk.
- Molecular and Genomic Integration: Employs markers like BRAF, RAS, and TERT promoter mutations to refine prognosis.
- Response-Based Stratification: Categorizes patients (Excellent to Structural Incomplete) based on Tg levels and imaging post-treatment.
- NIFTP Diagnostic Criteria: Requires strict histopathologic examination of the entire tumor capsule to identify non-invasive tumors.
- Lymph Node Density Assessment: Uses the ratio of metastatic to total examined lymph nodes to predict recurrence risk.
- Dynamic Risk Reassessment: Emphasizes continuous monitoring where risk is reassessed at every visit based on treatment response.
- PDTC Classification: Identifies aggressive tumors with solid/insular growth and necrosis or high mitotic activity.
- Family Screening Protocols: Recommends ultrasound screening for first-degree relatives in cases of multiple familial diagnoses.
- Contrast-Enhanced Imaging Strategy: Tailors the use of intravenous contrast based on individual patient risk and renal function.

**Notable Details:**
- DTC incidence has significantly increased, tripling from 1975 to 2015.
- Excellent response to therapy allows for de-escalation of monitoring due to low recurrence rates (<15%).
- Prophylactic lateral neck dissection is not recommended for cN0 patients.
- Sex is not considered an independent prognostic factor in ATA recurrence modeling.
- NIFTP requires the absence of papillae or psamm bodies.
- Risk assessment increasingly incorporates molecular profiles alongside traditional AJCC staging.
- Risk stratification is increasingly personalized, considering patient comorbidities and specific tumor subtypes.

## Genetic Testing Guidelines

These guidelines outline the integration of genetic testing in oncology, emphasizing the distinction between somatic and germline alterations, the clinical relevance of variant classification, and the structured approach to germline testing for hereditary cancer syndromes.

**Key Features:**
- Somatic vs. Germline Variant Detection: Differentiate between tumor-derived somatic mutations and potentially heritable germline variants during genomic testing.
- Multigene Panel Testing: Utilize broad testing panels to address overlapping phenotypes of hereditary predisposition conditions.
- Pre-Test Counseling & Informed Consent: Conduct comprehensive genetic counseling, including pedigree analysis, risk assessment, and psychosocial evaluation, prior to testing.
- Actionable Gene Prioritization: Focus germline testing on high-priority genes (e.g., BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1) with >5–10% tumor-to-germline conversion rates for variants with allele fractions >30%.

**Notable Details:**
- Germline testing is recommended for up to 40 genes if somatic mutations are identified in any tumor type.
- Seven genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PALB2, RET) are prioritized due to their high clinical actionability.
- Variant allele fraction >30% increases the likelihood of a germline origin for detected variants.
- Pre-test counseling must include a three- to four-generation pedigree analysis.
- Direct-to-consumer (DTC) germline testing requires adherence to the same pre-test counseling and informed consent standards as clinical testing.
- Hereditary cancer syndromes are evaluated through multigene panels due to overlapping phenotypic presentations.
- Clinical correlation with patient and family history is essential when germline variants are detected.
- Tumor-to-germline conversion rates vary by gene and variant type, influencing testing prioritization.

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Differentiate between angioinvasion and lymphatic invasion for synoptic reporting in tumor staging
- Vascular invasion is associated with increased risk of recurrence and distant metastasis in DTC

- Synoptic pathology reporting must include extranodal extension status (micro- or macro-) for accurate risk stratification

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** Novel Radiopharmaceuticals in DTC Detection

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- 124I PET/CT shows higher sensitivity than diagnostic planar WBS (99% vs. 66%) for detecting residual thyroid tissue or metastatic DTC, with pooled sensitivity of 94.2% and specificity of 49.0%.
- 18FDG-PET/CT is recommended for high-risk DTC patients with elevated serum Tg (typically >10 ng/mL), particularly those with negative RAI imaging or aggressive histologies (e.g., PDTC, tall cell cancers).
- 18FDG-PET/CT sensitivity is influenced by tumor de-differentiation, larger tumor burden, and serum Tg doubling time.
- 18FDG uptake in metastatic DTC is a negative prognostic factor for RAI response and survival, and may identify aggressive lesions warranting local treatment or intensive monitoring.
- False positives on 18FDG-PET/CT (0–39%) necessitate FNA biopsy with cytology and Tg measurement in accessible lymph nodes for confirmation.
- 124I PET/CT has not been compared with SPECT-CT in large patient series, limiting its validation against standard imaging modalities.
- 18FDG-PET/CT may identify lesions with high SUV uptake that are more aggressive, warranting local treatment or more intensive monitoring, even in the presence of detectable 131I uptake.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Patients must have high-risk DTC with elevated serum Tg levels (generally >10 ng/mL) or negative RAI imaging for 18FDG-PET/CT recommendation.
- Aggressive histologies (e.g., PDTC, tall cell cancers) or OTC warrant consideration for 18FDG-PET/CT.
- TSH stimulation does not improve 18FDG-PET/CT sensitivity and may increase false positives.
- Brain MRI and extended imaging to the feet are required if metastases to the brain or below the thighs are suspected.
- Aggressive pathological variants may produce only small amounts of serum Tg, making Tg levels less reliable for guiding 18FDG-PET/CT use in these cases.
- Detection of lesions in locations other than accessible lymph nodes should prompt dedicated cross-sectional imaging of those regions for confirmation and clinical decision-making.

### WHO Classification Updates

### Source 1: American Thyroid Association.pdf

**Clinical Recommendation:** WHO Classification Updates

**Key Clinical Findings:**
- Reclassification of noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features (NIFTP) as a benign entity rather than carcinoma.
- Introduction of the 'differentiated high-grade thyroid carcinoma' (DHGTC) category for aggressive variants of well-differentiated thyroid cancer.
- Integration of molecular markers (e.g., RAS, BRAF, PAX8-PPARg) into diagnostic criteria and prognostic stratification.
- Refinement of nomenclature for oncocytic tumors, distinguishing Hurthle cell neoplasms based on mitotic activity and necrosis.
- Adoption of updated TNM staging systems (AJCC 8th edition) incorporating molecular and histologic risk factors.
- Incorporation of telomere length and germline mutations (e.g., POT1, TINF2, CHEK2) into risk stratification frameworks for familial and sporadic thyroid cancers.
- Use of ultrasound criteria (e.g., TI-RADS) to guide fine-needle aspiration biopsy and reduce unnecessary procedures in low-risk nodules.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Pathologic confirmation of nuclear features consistent with papillary thyroid carcinoma variants.
- Assessment of mitotic count and presence of necrosis for high-grade differentiation.
- Molecular testing (e.g., RAS, BRAF, PAX8-PPARg) to support diagnosis of specific subtypes.
- Evaluation of extrathyroidal extension and lymph node involvement for staging.
- Differentiation between encapsulated and invasive growth patterns for follicular variants.
- Mandatory germline testing for patients with familial non-medullary thyroid cancer (FNMTC) to identify high-risk genetic variants (e.g., CHEK2, POT1).
- Preoperative ultrasonography to assess extrathyroidal extension and lymph node involvement for accurate TNM staging.
