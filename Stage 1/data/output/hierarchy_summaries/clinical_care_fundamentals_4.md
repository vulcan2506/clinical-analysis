# Clinical Care Fundamentals (4)

This category focuses on micronutrient deficiencies (B12 and folate), their clinical consequences, dietary sources, and targeted interventions for high-risk populations, emphasizing prevention and management strategies.

**Key Features:**
- Vitamin B12 deficiency: Causes irreversible neurological damage if untreated, with symptoms including anemia, cognitive impairment, and peripheral neuropathy.
- Folate in pregnancy: Critical for fetal neural development and red blood cell formation, with deficiency linked to neural tube defects and maternal anemia.
- Folate-rich dietary guidance: Recommends leafy greens, legumes, fortified foods, and fruits to meet folate needs.
- Folic acid preconception protocol: Mandates 5 mg daily folic acid supplementation starting 3 months preconception for individuals with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM).
- B12 dietary sources and vegan management: Emphasizes animal-based foods and fortified plant-based alternatives, with vegans advised to consume fortified foods at least 3 times daily.

**Notable Details:**
- Vitamin B12 deficiency can cause irreversible neurological damage if untreated.
- Folate deficiency in pregnancy increases the risk of neural tube defects (e.g., spina bifida) in the fetus.
- Folic acid supplementation at 5 mg/day is recommended for T1DM patients preconception due to higher baseline risk of neural tube defects.
- Vegans must rely on fortified foods or supplements for B12, as plant-based foods lack reliable natural sources.
- Conditions like coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, and alcoholism can impair folate absorption, necessitating targeted supplementation.
- Symptoms of B12 deficiency include fatigue, glossitis (sore/red tongue), and peripheral neuropathy.
- Folate-rich foods include Brussels sprouts, spinach, chickpeas, and fortified breakfast cereals.
- B12-rich foods include red meat, shellfish, eggs, dairy, and fortified plant-based milks.

### vitamin B12 essential functions and deficiency

Vitamin B12 is necessary for making red blood cells, DNA, maintaining nerve cell health, and releasing energy from food.. Deficiency symptoms include anaemia, fatigue, sore/red tongue, muscle weakness, nerve damage, depression, and memory loss.

### Source 1: Dietary advice for Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.pdf

**Nutrient Deficiency:** vitamin B12 essential functions and deficiency

**Risk Factors and Clinical Manifestations:**
- Vitamin B12 is necessary for making red blood cells, DNA, maintaining nerve cell health, and releasing energy from food.
- Deficiency symptoms include anaemia, fatigue, sore/red tongue, muscle weakness, nerve damage, depression, and memory loss.
- Pernicious anaemia destroys stomach cells, preventing B12 absorption and requiring lifelong injections.
- Reduced stomach acid (e.g., in older adults or due to acid-suppressing medications) impairs B12 absorption.
- Intestinal surgeries or digestive disorders (e.g., Crohn's disease, coeliac disease) increase deficiency risk.
- Vitamin B12 is naturally found in animal foods (e.g., meat, fish, dairy, eggs) and can be obtained from fortified foods or supplements.

**Patient Preparation / Method:**
- Patients on acid-suppressing medications may need fortified foods or supplements for adequate B12 intake.
- Individuals with pernicious anaemia require lifelong B12 injections for treatment.
- Patients with suspected pernicious anaemia should undergo testing for intrinsic factor antibodies or undergo a Schilling test to confirm diagnosis.


### folate in pregnancy and anaemia

Folic acid is a B-class vitamin essential for red blood cell formation and fetal neural development.. Folate deficiency can cause anaemia and may result from poor dietary intake or conditions like coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, or alcoholism.

### Source 1: Dietary advice for Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.pdf

**Nutrient Deficiency:** folate in pregnancy and anaemia

**Risk Factors and Clinical Manifestations:**
- Folic acid is a B-class vitamin essential for red blood cell formation and fetal neural development.
- Folate deficiency can cause anaemia and may result from poor dietary intake or conditions like coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, or alcoholism.
- Pregnant women should take folic acid supplements in the first 12 weeks of pregnancy to reduce neural tube defect risks.
- Folic acid supplementation is recommended 2–3 months before conception to build protective levels.
- Folate demand increases during lactation due to maternal nutrient transfer to breast milk.

**Patient Preparation / Method:**
- Screen for folate deficiency in high-risk groups (e.g., untreated coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, alcoholism).
- Assess dietary folate intake and absorption capacity before supplementation.
- Assess folate status in women planning pregnancy or with a history of neural tube defects in prior pregnancies.


### folate-rich dietary guidance

Consume folate-rich foods such as Brussels sprouts, broccoli, leafy greens (kale, coriander, spring greens, spinach, cabbage), cooked beans (black-eyed/kidney beans, chickpeas), fortified breakfast cereals, fruits (oranges, banana, grapefruit, kiwi), yeast, kidney, and beef extracts.

### Source 1: Dietary advice for Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.pdf

**Nutrient Deficiency:** folate-rich dietary guidance

**Risk Factors and Clinical Manifestations:**
- Consume folate-rich foods such as Brussels sprouts, broccoli, leafy greens (kale, coriander, spring greens, spinach, cabbage), cooked beans (black-eyed/kidney beans, chickpeas), fortified breakfast cereals, fruits (oranges, banana, grapefruit, kiwi), yeast, kidney, and beef extracts.
- Avoid overcooking vegetables to prevent folate loss; prefer steaming or microwaving.
- Do not keep foods hot for long periods to preserve folate content.
- Consume raw or lightly cooked fruits (e.g., oranges, bananas) to maximize folate retention.

- Folic acid supplementation with a daily dose of 5 mg should be started 3 months before planned conception.

- Planned conception timing must be assessed to initiate folic acid supplementation 3 months prior.

**Risk Factors and Clinical Manifestations:**
- Consume vitamin B12 rich foods including red meat, fish, shellfish, eggs, poultry, dairy, yeast extract, fortified cereals, tofu, and plant-based milks.
- Eat a varied and balanced diet to maintain adequate B12 levels.
- Vegans should aim to eat foods fortified with vitamin B12 at least 3 times a day.
- If fortified foods are inadequate, take a vitamin B12 supplement of 10 micrograms per day as recommended by the Vegan Society.
