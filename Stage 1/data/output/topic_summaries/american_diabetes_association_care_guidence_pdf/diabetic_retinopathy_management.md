# diabetic retinopathy management
*Source: American Diabetes Association Care Guidence.pdf*

**Clinical Recommendation:** Diabetic retinopathy management

**Core Recommendations:**
- Implement strategies to help people with diabetes reach glycemic, blood pressure, and lipid goals to reduce the risk or slow the progression of diabetic retinopathy.
- Perform initial dilated and comprehensive eye examinations based on diabetes type and duration (5 years after type 1 diabetes onset; at diagnosis for type 2 diabetes).
- Use retinal photography with remote reading or FDA-approved AI algorithms for screening, with pathways for timely referral to ophthalmologists.
- Refer individuals with diabetic macular edema, moderate or worse nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy, or proliferative diabetic retinopathy to an ophthalmologist.
- Use intravitreal anti-VEGF injections as first-line treatment for most diabetic macular edema involving the fovea and for proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
- Assess retinopathy status when intensifying glucose-lowering therapies (e.g., GLP-1 RAs) due to risk of initial worsening of retinopathy with rapid A1C reductions.
- Use macular focal/grid photocoagulation or intravitreal corticosteroid injections for persistent diabetic macular edema despite prior anti-VEGF therapy or in non-candidates for anti-VEGF.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Glycemic goals must be individualized based on patient-specific factors (e.g., life expectancy, hypoglycemia risk).
- Blood pressure and lipid management should be optimized to reduce retinopathy progression.
- Retinal photography or AI screening requires validation and pathways for referral to comprehensive eye exams.
- Pregnant individuals with preexisting diabetes require pre-pregnancy eye exams and trimester-specific monitoring.
- Prompt management of infections and use of advanced therapies (e.g., WIFI staging) for diabetic foot complications.
- Retinopathy screening in children and adolescents with type 1 or type 2 diabetes (see section 14, 'Children and Adolescents').
- Adjust screening intervals based on specific risk factors (e.g., not meeting glycemic goals, advanced retinopathy, or diabetic macular edema) for more frequent examinations.

**Previous Recommendations:**
- Fixed numeric A1C targets for older adults.
- Reliance solely on retinal photography without pathways for comprehensive eye exams.
- Use of panretinal laser photocoagulation as the only treatment for proliferative diabetic retinopathy without considering anti-VEGF alternatives.
- Aspirin contraindication in individuals with retinopathy due to retinal hemorrhage risk.
- Monotherapy with laser for center-involved diabetic macular edema without considering anti-VEGF agents.

**Updated Recommendations:**
- Expanded discussion of GLP-1 RA effects on eye health, including potential risks (e.g., mild worsening of retinopathy) and benefits (e.g., reduced intraocular pressure, lower glaucoma risk).
- FDA-approved AI platforms (AEYE-DS, EyeArt, LumineticsCore) for diabetic retinopathy screening.
- Use of intravitreal anti-VEGF agents as alternatives to panretinal laser photocoagulation for proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
- Emphasis on holistic, multifactorial approaches to eye health in diabetes, including adjunctive therapies.
- Inclusion of emerging technologies (e.g., self-monitoring foot temperatures) for early identification of diabetic foot complications.
- FDA pregnancy category C classification for anti-VEGF medications, warranting caution in pregnant individuals with diabetes due to theoretical fetal vascular risks.
- Use of fenofibrate in individuals with dyslipidemia to slow retinopathy progression, particularly with early diabetic retinopathy at baseline.
