# CGM device classification and usage
*Source: American Diabetes Association Care Guidence.pdf*

**Clinical Recommendation:** CGM device classification and usage

**Core Recommendations:**
- CGM devices are categorized as rtCGM, isCGM, OTC-CGM, or professional CGM based on data stream, alarms, and user requirements.
- Frequency of sensor use in type 1 diabetes is a predictor of A1C lowering across all age-groups.
- Most rtCGM systems require minimal or no user calibration, but all CGM systems require BGM confirmation in cases of perceived discordance.
- CGM benefits include reduced A1C, hypoglycemia episodes, and acute diabetes complications, with consistent use yielding the greatest improvements.
- CGM use in type 2 diabetes (insulin or noninsulin) improves TIR, reduces hyperglycemia, and enhances user satisfaction, though insulin dose changes are often unnecessary.
- Reductions in acute diabetes complications (e.g., DKA, severe hypoglycemia, diabetes-related coma) and hospitalizations for hypoglycemia/hyperglycemia persist for at least 2 years after CGM initiation in adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes
- isCGM devices show glycemic benefits in type 1 and type 2 diabetes across multiple therapy types (MDI, basal insulin, noninsulin) based on observational, retrospective, and registry data

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- Prescription required for rtCGM devices used in diabetes management.
- OTC-CGM devices can be purchased by anyone, including those without diabetes or with prediabetes, but lack alarms and alerts.
- BGM confirmation is required for all CGM systems when sensor readings conflict with clinical perception.
- Integration with digitally connected devices (e.g., insulin systems) depends on device-specific compatibility.
- Patient willingness and ability to regularly wear and interpret CGM data are critical for sustained benefits.
- Healthcare professional oversight is recommended for professional CGM use to ensure proper data interpretation and application
- Behavioral support for parents of young children with type 1 diabetes using CGM is advised to reduce hypoglycemia concerns and diabetes distress

**Previous Recommendations:**
- CGM systems requiring frequent user calibration (e.g., older rtCGM models with mandatory fingerstick calibration).
- Reliance solely on BGM for glucose monitoring in patients eligible for CGM.
- Fixed A1C targets without consideration of CGM-derived metrics (e.g., TIR, time below/above range).
- Use of professional CGM devices without healthcare professional oversight or data interpretation.
- Assumption that CGM benefits are limited to type 1 diabetes or insulin-treated patients.

**Updated Recommendations:**
- OTC-CGM devices for lifestyle assessment in individuals without diabetes or with prediabetes.
- Integration of CGM with automated insulin systems or connected insulin pens as a class II medical device (iCGM).
- Real-world evidence showing reduced acute diabetes complications (DKA, severe hypoglycemia) with CGM use.
- CGM use in type 2 diabetes (insulin or noninsulin) demonstrating improved TIR and reduced hyperglycemia without mandatory insulin dose adjustments.
- Observational data supporting rtCGM benefits in adults with type 2 diabetes not on insulin, including reduced mean glucose and GMI.
- Short-term CGM use in youth with type 2 diabetes may not immediately impact glucose metrics or A1C but can lead to behavioral changes (e.g., increased BGM, insulin administration) and improved diabetes management and quality of life
