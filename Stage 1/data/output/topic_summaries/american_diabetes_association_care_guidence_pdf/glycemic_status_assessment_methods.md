# glycemic status assessment methods
*Source: American Diabetes Association Care Guidence.pdf*

**Clinical Recommendation:** Glycemic status assessment methods

**Core Recommendations:**
- A1C is the primary tool for assessing glycemic status, reflecting average glycemia over 2–3 months and strongly linked to diabetes complications.
- CGM metrics (TIR, TBR, TAR, GMI, coefficient of variation, mean glucose) provide nuanced glucose data for self-management and treatment optimization.
- BGM via capillary (finger-stick) devices remains integral for insulin-treated individuals and complements A1C and CGM.
- Glycemic status should be assessed at least twice yearly, with more frequent monitoring (e.g., every 3 months) for unstable glucose levels, recent treatment changes, or hypoglycemia/hyperglycemia.
- A1C testing should be performed routinely at initial assessment and continuing care, with point-of-care testing offering timely treatment adjustments.
- CGM is increasingly important for optimizing treatment in people with diabetes in pregnancy and other forms of diabetes (e.g., cystic fibrosis-related diabetes).
- A1C does not provide a measure of glycemic variability, real-time glucose levels, or hypoglycemia, necessitating complementary BGM or CGM data for comprehensive assessment.

**Patient Eligibility Criteria:**
- A1C testing requires NGSP-certified assays for accuracy and should be performed in all people with diabetes.
- Alternative monitoring (e.g., fructosamine, glycated albumin, BGM, CGM) is required when A1C interpretation is compromised (e.g., hemoglobinopathies, anemia, pregnancy).
- CGM wear of 70% or higher is recommended for reliable TIR assessment over 10–14 days.
- Individuals with type 1 diabetes, insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, or high hypoglycemia risk benefit most from CGM.
- Discordant A1C and CGM/BGM results require further evaluation due to potential inaccuracies or high glycemic variability.
- A1C testing should be performed routinely at initial assessment and as part of continuing care in all people with diabetes.
- Alternative glycemic monitoring (e.g., fructosamine, glycated albumin, BGM, CGM) is required when A1C interpretation is compromised due to conditions affecting red blood cell turnover (e.g., hemolytic anemia, anemia, recent blood transfusion, kidney failure, pregnancy) or homozygous hemoglobin variants (e.g., HbSS, HbEE).

**Previous Recommendations:**
- Use of fructosamine or glycated albumin as primary glycemic monitoring tools without clear indications (e.g., A1C interference).
- Relying solely on A1C for glycemic variability assessment without complementary BGM or CGM data.
- Fixed numeric A1C targets without consideration of patient-specific factors (e.g., hypoglycemia risk, life expectancy).
- Point-of-care A1C testing as a substitute for laboratory assays in all clinical scenarios due to potential accuracy limitations.
- Assuming race/ethnicity as a proxy for A1C interpretation differences.

**Updated Recommendations:**
- CGM metrics (TIR, TBR, TAR, GMI) as standardized tools for glycemic status assessment and clinical decision-making.
- 10–14 day CGM wear periods with ≥70% wear time for reliable TIR and other metric evaluation.
- Remote glucose data analysis to guide real-time diabetes therapy adjustments.
- Integration of GMI and coefficient of variation (>36%) into treatment plans for hypoglycemia risk assessment.
- Emphasis on individualized glycemic monitoring frequency based on treatment stability, hypoglycemia risk, and life stage.
- Reporting of estimated average glucose (eAG) alongside A1C results to provide context for glycemic control interpretation.
- Standardized CGM metric reports with visual summaries to facilitate clinical interpretation and treatment decision-making.
